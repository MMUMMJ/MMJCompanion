1. Welcome to MMJ

Starting university means learning a new subject, a new way of working and a new set of expectations at the same time.

University is different

You will have more independence. You will gradually take responsibility for organising your learning, tracking deadlines and asking for help when needed.

Everyone feels uncertain

Some students will look as though they know exactly what they are doing. They may not. Uncertainty is a normal part of starting university.

Journalism is exciting but demanding

You will research, interview, write, record, photograph, edit and produce work for real audiences. Sometimes you will do things before you feel completely ready.

Your confidence will develop

You do not need to arrive confident. Confidence develops through practice, feedback, reflection and trying again.

You are here to learn.

Making mistakes, asking questions, receiving feedback and trying again are all part of becoming a journalist.

2. Understanding University

Things you need to know about how university works and what lecturers actually expect in first year.

Starting outWhat do lecturers actually expect?
Lecturers do not expect you to know everything. They do expect you to attend and participate, prepare for teaching, check the information provided to you, keep track of deadlines, attempt work independently, ask questions when you are unsure and respond constructively to feedback.
Study habitsIndependent learning

Independent learning does not mean sitting alone for hours trying to work everything out. It means taking responsibility for what you need to learn: reading, practising, researching, checking, preparing and asking focused questions when you are stuck.

Try first. Ask second. Follow up third.

TeachingLectures
Lectures introduce ideas, concepts, examples and ways of thinking. You do not need to copy every word. Listen for the main ideas, key examples, things you do not understand and topics you want to explore further.
TeachingSeminars and workshops
This is where you get to use what you are learning. You may discuss ideas, analyse journalism, practise techniques, work with other students, produce something or receive feedback. You will get more from these sessions if you participate rather than simply attend.
Getting helpHow to contact your tutors
Before contacting a lecturer, check the assessment brief, Moodle and any guidance already provided. Then explain who you are, which module or assessment you mean, what you have checked and your specific question.
Getting helpAsking questions
There are no stupid questions. Learning to distinguish between “I haven't checked” and “I've checked and I still don't understand” is part of becoming an independent learner.
Your workAcademic integrity

Your work needs to be your own. Be honest about where information comes from, whose ideas you use, who you interview and what material you create. If you are unsure whether something is acceptable, ask before submitting.

Your responsibilitiesAttendance

Journalism is practical and collaborative. Missing teaching can mean missing instruction, discussion, practice, feedback, group activity or assessment information. If you cannot attend, follow the appropriate university process and find out what you have missed.

Your systemsUniversity systems
Get into the habit of checking university email, Moodle, assessment information, your timetable, programme announcements and any other platform used by your modules. Put important dates into your own calendar.
Your responsibilitiesWhy nobody reminds you about everything
Part of becoming an independent student is learning to monitor your own deadlines and responsibilities. Do not rely on someone reminding you. Use your own calendar and check the information available to you.

3. Understanding Journalism

The habits you develop now will become part of your identity as a journalist.

How journalists think

Start with: What don't I know yet? Good journalism comes from curiosity, questions and a willingness to challenge assumptions.

Be curious

Ask what is happening, why it matters, who is affected, who might disagree and what you still need to find out.

Verification

Someone saying something does not make it true. A social media post or press release is not automatically the whole story. If you do not know, check.

News judgement

Think about significance, relevance, impact, public interest, proximity, timeliness, conflict and novelty. Practice develops judgement.

Professional behaviour

Be prepared, reliable, punctual, respectful, organised, accurate, responsive and willing to learn. People remember whether you were trustworthy.

Ethics

Think about accuracy, fairness, privacy, consent, harm, representation, vulnerability and public interest. Sometimes there will not be an obvious answer.

Digital identity

Your online presence can become part of your professional identity. Think about what your public social media activity communicates about you.

Working with sources

Keep track of who you spoke to, their contact details, what they told you, when you spoke and what still needs checking. Do not rely on memory.

Developing confidence

Confidence comes from preparation and practice, not pretending you know more than you do.

Confidence with technology

You do not need to be a technical expert when you start. Your phone can already help you record interviews, take photographs, shoot video, research and capture notes effectively.

Journalists do not need to know everything.

They need to know what they need to find out, how to find it, how to check it and how to explain it clearly.

5. Your First Assessments

Assessment can feel complicated at first. Break it down into manageable steps.

  1. Read the brief properly.

    Identify the task, target audience, format, word/time limit, required elements, exact deadline and submission method.

  2. Understand the marking criteria.

    Use the rubric to understand what successful work needs to demonstrate. Refer to it while producing the work, not just afterwards.

  3. Plan backwards.

    Work backwards from the deadline through editing, checking, production, research and preparation. Set an earlier personal deadline.

  4. Break the task into actions.

    Replace “do assignment” with specific actions such as research, contact sources, interview, write draft, edit and final check.

  5. Check before submitting.

    Return to the brief and criteria. Double check facts, names, audio links, spelling, file formats and submission location.

  6. Learn from the result.

    Use feedback to decide what you will carry forward and what specific adjustments you will make next time.

Common first-year mistake:

Starting with the creative idea before establishing what the assessment actually requires. Understand the task first. Then decide how to make the work interesting.

6. Feedback Recovery Guide

A process for pausing, understanding what your feedback is really telling you and deciding what to do differently next time.

1. PAUSE

Manage reaction

Give yourself time before deciding what the mark means.

2. UNDERSTAND

Read criteria

Identify what worked and what most urgently needs improvement.

3. TRANSLATE

Make actions

Turn abstract comments into concrete next steps.

4. ACT

Create plan

Choose 2–3 changes for next time. Don't fix everything at once.

5. CONFIDENCE

Keep perspective

One mark evaluates one piece of work. It is not an overall judgement about your potential as a student or journalist.

7. Deadline Toolkit

Practical tools for managing assignments and recovering when things do not go to plan.

1. Write everything down

Get assignments, deadlines, practical work, interviews, meetings and personal commitments out of your head and onto a clear list.

2. Identify immediate priority

Ask what genuinely needs your attention first. The task you are most worried about is not always the most urgent.

3. Break it down

Turn “I have an assignment” into small actions such as finding sources, preparing questions, interviewing and editing.

4. Work in short blocks

Try 25–30 minutes working focused, then 5 minutes away from the screen. Repeat as needed.

5. Remove friction

Put your phone away, close unnecessary tabs, and prepare your recording equipment or document beforehand.

6. If you are behind, act

Do not disappear. Work out what is outstanding, what is due first, what is realistic and who you need to contact.

9. Questions Everyone Asks

Instant answers to common first-year questions and doubts.

BelongingIs everyone else coping better than me?
You are seeing what other people show you, not what they are experiencing privately. Some students struggle with workload, others with confidence, belonging or understanding assessments. You need to focus on your next practical step.
AssessmentWhat if I hate my first assignment?
Do not make a major judgement about yourself or the course based on one assessment. Work out whether the problem is the topic, format, journalism, uncertainty or workload, then decide what you need to do next.
AssessmentWhat if I don't understand my first assignment?
Start with the brief. Identify the exact point you do not understand and ask your module leader a specific question.
FeedbackWhat if I fail an assignment?
Do not disappear. Read the feedback, understand what needs to change and find out what happens next under current university assessment rules.
Getting helpShould I email lecturers?
Yes, when you need to ask an appropriate question or need support. Check Moodle and the brief first, then make your question specific and clear.
FeedbackWhat if I don't understand my feedback?
Use the Feedback Recovery Guide. If you are still unsure, ask your tutor for clarification on specific comments.
Study habitsHow many hours should I be working?
University study involves scheduled teaching plus preparation, reading, research, journalism practice, production, assessment and reflection. The balance varies. The key is using your time deliberately rather than waiting for deadlines.
WellbeingIs it normal to feel overwhelmed?
Feeling overwhelmed can happen, particularly during periods of change or assessment. “Normal” does not mean you should simply put up with it. If it is affecting your ability to study or function, talk to someone.
BelongingWhat if I don't have any friends yet?
It takes time. Start small: talk before class, sit with someone new, join group activities, ask someone about an assignment or take part in programme activities. Friendships often develop through repeated contact.
JournalismDo journalists need to know everything?
No. Journalism depends on curiosity, asking questions, researching and verifying information.
Your courseCan I change my mind about studying Multimedia Journalism?
You can always talk to someone if you are unsure about your course. Speak to your Personal Tutor or Programme Lead before making a decision so you understand all your options.
Getting helpCan I ask “stupid” questions?
Yes. You will usually learn more by asking than by pretending you understand something you do not.

WorkloadI'm falling behind.

List everything outstanding. Find the nearest important deadline. Break the assessment into small actions. Decide what you can realistically complete today. Identify whether you need help. Make a realistic plan for the next few days.

If the problem is bigger than one deadline, speak to your Personal Tutor.

AssessmentI don't understand what my assignment is asking.

Read the brief again and highlight the main instruction, required elements, format, word/time limit, deadline and marking criteria. Write down the one specific thing you still do not understand and ask your module leader.

UniversityEveryone else seems to understand university except me.

University has expectations and routines that are not always explained directly. Check whether you know where to find information, understand your assessments, know who to ask, are tracking deadlines and are attending teaching. If one of these is unclear, that is something you can easily learn.

Your courseI'm thinking about withdrawing.

Talk to your Personal Tutor or Programme Lead before making a final decision. Work out whether the issue is workload, finances, wellbeing, confidence, belonging, a particular assessment or uncertainty about the course. There may be support or another option you have not considered.

AttendanceI can't attend university.

Follow the appropriate attendance/reporting process. Check what teaching you will miss, check Moodle, find out what you need to catch up on and speak to your tutor if you are unsure. If this is happening repeatedly, talk to your Personal Tutor.

JournalismI'm struggling with a journalism story.

Journalism can involve difficult material, including grief, trauma, violence, abuse and distressing testimony. Being affected does not mean you are not suited to journalism. Speak to your tutor if you are struggling with a particular story or assignment, and use appropriate university wellbeing support if the issue is affecting your wider wellbeing.
